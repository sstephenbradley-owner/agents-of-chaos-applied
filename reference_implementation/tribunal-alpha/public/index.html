Tribunal

The audit layer for writing, whether you used AI or not.

About this project: what it is, how to use it, what it's for

Tribunal fact-checks written work by refusing to let one model grade its own homework. Instead of asking a single model "is this accurate?", it splits the job across separate calls that deliberately know different things:

  • Drafter reads the text and pulls out the claims a reader could actually check.
  • Adversary receives those claims stripped of all context (no source text, no author to defend them) and argues why each might be wrong. Isolation is the point: it can't be swayed by surrounding prose or by an argument it already agreed with.
  • Reference searches the live record. It's the only node that can see past its own training cutoff, which is how the pipeline avoids confidently calling a recent true event a fabrication.
  • Judge sees everything and rules, weighing a sourced finding above an unsourced objection.

Each node's blindness is deliberate. A model that sees the whole conversation tends to agree with it.

How to use it

  1. Open Keys and models and paste an API key: Anthropic, Google Gemini, or both. Keys go from your browser to this site's function to the provider on each request and are never stored on the server. "Remember" keeps them in your browser only.
  2. Pick a mode. One provider for everything, or multi-model, which assigns each node to a different model so the Judge never grades its own Drafter.
  3. Give it something to read: a link, a PDF or Word file, or pasted text.
  4. Tick Academic mode for a manuscript (see below). Leave it off for essays, articles, reports, and posts.
  5. Press Audit. Each claim comes back with the ruling, the reasoning, the attack against it, and whatever the record said.

What it's for

General writing. Essays, journalism, op-eds, reports, marketing copy, grant prose: anywhere a confident sentence might be carrying a number, a date, or an attribution that nobody checked. It's as useful on human writing as on AI-assisted drafts; the failure mode it catches (fluent, specific, unverified) is one people produce on their own.

Academic mode. Built for manuscripts. The Reference node searches the literature rather than the open web, and every source's publication year is resolved against PubMed and Crossref so that work published after your paper can never be used as prior art against it. An Evidence Appraiser reads your manuscript and rates the strength of its own evidence (design, controls, sample size, whether a causal claim is resting on correlational data), scoped to what the document form actually reports, so an abstract isn't penalised for omitting statistics. Each claim is then ruled on three independent axes: is it true, is it new, and is it evidenced. A finding can be sound and unoriginal, or original and thinly supported, and those deserve different sentences in a revision letter.

A claim that departs from the published literature is not thereby wrong; novel results are supposed to depart from it. That's why the evidence axis exists separately from the truth axis.

What it won't do

  • Paywalled and login-walled pages won't fetch. Paste the text instead.
  • Literature checks reach abstracts reliably and full text only when it's open access.
  • Provider safety filters sometimes refuse a legitimate manuscript outright. This has been seen with select-agent microbiology. The run stops and says so.
  • It doesn't yet read your unpublished data and check your results against it. That's next.
  • It is an alpha, and its rulings are a prompt for your judgment, not a substitute for it. Verify anything before you act on it.
